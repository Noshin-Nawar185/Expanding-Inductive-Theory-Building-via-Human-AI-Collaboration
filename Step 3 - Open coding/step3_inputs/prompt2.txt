Start with no codebook and let the labels come from the material. Once you have labels, use the same label wherever it fits, even if its words came from another turn, and create a new label only when none of yours fits; your labels so far are listed below, each with its definition. A label fits a passage that says the same thing, even in other words: in a study like the guide's example of war veterans, if one veteran says he "hardened" and another later says he "stopped feeling anything", "hardened" may fit both. But a label fits only when its definition is true of what the member says, without stretching it. One label stands for one idea, not one topic: if one member calls his side projects a welcome break and another calls them a strain, they need different labels, even though both talk about side projects, because a label broad enough to fit both names only the topic. If you would have to stretch a label, create a new one instead. In later steps your codes will be compared across all 52 acts, and labels that name the same idea in the same way are what make that comparison possible. So before you create a label, check your list for one that already names the same idea in other words, and use it if there is one: as it grows, most of your codes should reuse a label you already have, and new labels should become rare. Published guidance puts a whole study at about 50 to 300 labels, and never in the thousands; your list is for all 52 acts of this study, not for one. Reusing a label in this way is not merging codes: do not merge labels you already have, or turn them into themes, as that comes in later steps.
Label a code with the members' own words wherever a phrase they say can reasonably be a label: if someone says "I was scared", then "scared" is a better label than "fear". A code is "most often a word or short phrase" (Saldaña, The Coding Manual for Qualitative Researchers) that captures the essence of a passage: a name for what the member means, which a reader would understand without seeing the turn, not a quotation. So choose a phrase of theirs that carries the idea, usually one to five words, not one that only repeats striking words; never a whole sentence or clause, and never parts of a turn joined with "..."; the quotation belongs in the evidence. Test every label as if it stood alone in a long list of labels from all 52 acts: would a reader know what it names? A word that makes sense only with the turn in view fails the test, as "happened" would for a member who says the band's look "just happened, we never planned it". Then build the label on their words, adding what it is about, as in "never-planned look", or, if nothing they say works, use a few plain words of your own; there is no quota for either. Whatever its words, a label must be true to what the member says: it may shorten their words or name their point, but it must not add a cause, motive, feeling or judgement that the passage does not show, nor leave out a "not", a "but" or a change on which their point rests. The research question is about change over time, so watch for change within what a member says, as in "at first … then …" or "we used to … now …". Where the point of a passage is a change, the label names the change, not one side of it: a member who says the band argued constantly at first and then learnt to leave each other alone is describing "learning to leave each other alone", not "arguing". Name what the member says about something, not only the topic: "side projects" names a topic, while "torn between two bands" names what the member says about it. A good label can apply to other passages, in this act and in others. When you first use a label, give it a definition, as described below; for a label in your own words, the definition also says why no phrase of theirs works. Whether a label uses the members' words exactly is worked out for you: if you change their wording, even slightly, the label counts as your own.
